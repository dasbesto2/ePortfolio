Unit 1 — Introduction to Machine Learning

LO1 Ethics & professionalismLO4 Team & professional practice

This unit opened the module's team project (group allocation) and the first collaborative discussion, on the societal stakes of autonomous systems.

Discussion 1: The 4th Industrial Revolution

The thread used the Boeing 737 MAX disasters as a case study in what happens when an autonomous system fails in a safety-critical setting. My summary post, below, draws together my initial post and three peers' responses (Rafaela Cavalcante, Dzemal Jukic, John-Jon Steyn).

I began the discussion using the Boeing 737 Max (Leggett, 2019) tragedy to illustrate how catastrophic failures in autonomous systems result in irreversible physical, financial, and reputational damage, stressing that Industry 5.0 must implement frameworks that prioritise human welfare. Rafaela Cavalcante expanded on this by emphasizing that reliability requires making correct decisions within strict timing constraints (Gupta et al., 2026), mentioning resilience as an essential safety component and advocating for a harm-reduction governance approach (Sen et al., 2026). Dzemal Jukic then focused on the human element, citing the ironies of automation (Bainbridge, 1983), where systems remove routine control but require operators to successfully manage rare, complex emergencies. He concludes that Industry 5.0 should focus on building systems that support human understanding rather than treat operators as simple backups, which often turns out to be faulty.

The ironies Dzemal raised are expanded by Strauch (2018), who argues that adding functionality beyond what operators need can disguise performance shortcomings until a crisis occurs. Torres (2026) links this to agency erosion, proposing that normative directionality — the human's domain understanding — must remain above measurable thresholds to prevent operators from becoming passive nodes. To operationalise the "meaningful human control" the discussion pointed towards, Lou (2025) defines Human-in-the-Loop (HitL) for unit-level situational awareness and Human-on-the-Loop (HotL) for system-level oversight; Lazaros (2026) explains that HitL requires direct participation in every decision for high-risk tasks, while HotL facilitates shared control. These architectures also speak to Rafaela's resilience concerns, balancing automated speed with human judgement inside Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS).

Reflecting on this dialogue, my peers' insights pushed me to look deeper into the literature — moving from Dzemal's automation-ironies point to the technical HitL/HotL distinction was particularly enlightening, and the process helped the group turn abstract safety concerns into actionable design principles.
